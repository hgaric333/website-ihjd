How has Colonialism shaped the International Political Economy?

Hermina Garic · May 24, 2022 · 2 min read

The international political economy or global economy is often referred to in scholarship with reference to either the political or economic aspects of its construction. However, what is essentially dismissed from this understanding of the international political economy is how colonialism has shaped and sustained it. So, how has colonialism shaped the international political economy?

To answer this question we must first understand the framing of its name. When we refer to the international political economy it is easy to claim that only political or economic interests shape its meaning. For example, many scholars studying the international political economy conceptualize the political side pertaining to the “pursuit of power and influence by a wide range of public and private actors. With public actors being referred to the state which is a sovereign or territorial political unit such as a country.” Additionally, the economic side pertains to the “pursuit of wealth and prosperity in the market, a coordinating mechanism where buyers and sellers exchange goods and services at prices determined by supply and demand.”

These two conceptualizations are often referenced as the norm or accepted theories. The economic literature is often limited to dialogue or discussions that include the stock market, trade agreements, currencies and so on. While the political literature is often limited to discussions that include whether or not governments intervene in global economic crises, which businesses they regulate and how they keep or enforce economic conditions through international institutions such as the World Bank.

Anything challenging that norm is referred to as a critique of the international political economy instead of the established economic structure.

However, what this does is often dismiss the scholarship of individuals who reference the perspective of the global economy from those who have experienced colonization. Much of this scholarship establishes that capitalism would not have been sustainable nor affordable without the institution of slavery, and that the transfers of wealth from colonized lands to established colonial powers continue to shape existing social, political, and economic inequities. Therefore, scholars reference and view the global economy as a colonial global economy.

It is significant to understand the development of the colonial global economy through the conditions in which it was born. The international political economy we see today is built on the foundations of a global capitalist economy which was never separate from colonization even though colonization is often referenced as something that occurred prior to capitalism. We can disrupt this narrative by paying attention to the fact that colonialism is continued today through post-colonialism, much of what is sustained today through corporate entities, international trade agreements and political institutions that oversee the development of formally colonized lands, all of which are essential to the functions of a colonial global economy.
