What Nigeria's Colonial History can teach us about the End SARs Movement

Hermina Garic · February 19, 2021 · 4 min read · Updated May 18, 2022

The Special Anti-Robbery Squad, SARs is portrayed as a police unit dedicated to fighting crime. However, activists highlight that SARs is a rogue terror agency with a mission to extract money from and terrorize citizens. Prominent political voices such as Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie have noted that SARs officers have not been held accountable for the crimes committed against citizens since the inception of the agency.

The most prominent abuses occurred against citizens on October 20, 2020. The Lagos state governor announced a curfew after two weeks of peaceful protests. During this curfew, the Lekki Toll Gate security cameras and bright floodlights were cut off. Soldiers walked toward protestors that were holding Nigerian flags, sitting on the ground, and singing the national anthem. The soldiers were ordered to shoot into the crowd of people. As Adichie highlights, this violence against citizens was state sanctioned.

How did Nigeria get here? In order to understand the End SARS movement, we need to frame the political conversation within a historical understanding of Nigeria’s development.

The state of Nigeria’s economy, the rogue nature and ruthless violence of SARs against citizens, and the demands of End SARs protestors trace back to unresolved political and economic issues stemming from European colonization.

A country administered as a business

First, it is significant to challenge the notion that Nigeria was established as an independent political entity. As Benjamin Maiangwa argues, British colonists did not “conceive of or organize Nigeria as a nation, but rather it was administered as a business enterprise in which the Crown depended on companies to govern its Nigerian colonies.” One example of this is the creation of the United Africa Company in 1879 which became the Royal Niger Company in 1886. The Royal Niger Company eventually changed its name to The Niger Company Ltd. and it was later absorbed by the Unilever Company. Unilever is still a British owned multinational company.

How far did the global community actually stray from colonialism? Not much. It is significant to note that The Niger Company established both commercial and governing rights over the territories of the Niger area and built a military force to ensure its survival as well as control of the area. Colonial rule switched from direct rule to indirect rule in 1914 when the Northern and Southern protectorates or colonies of Niger were consolidated into one Nigeria. The military force was then expanded to ensure the survival and control of Nigeria as a business state. Maiangwa thus theorizes that the organized violence which we are witnessing with SARs today was made possible because businesses drove the colonial process that gave rise to the existence of Nigeria.

An economy built for export

Today, Nigeria's economy exists as heavily export-based and the oil industry is its most lucrative. Nigeria was considered one of two of the wealthiest economies in Africa in 2018, but it was also home to the world’s greatest concentration of extreme poverty. This is because its economy is heavily reliant on the sale and production of oil. It is also important to note that most of the profits come from European countries such as Spain and the Netherlands, who were one of the largest importers of Nigeria’s crude oil and condensate in 2019.

Additionally, as mentioned earlier, Unilever still operates in Nigeria. Another abundant resource that is found in Nigeria that is essential to many of the products that Unilever sells is palm oil. The fruit that is used to make palm oil is grown on plantations in Nigeria. While it is an abundant resource that has the potential to be another basis for Nigeria’s economy, it is being controlled by the British global giant and all the profits from it are outsourced from the country.

So what does this all mean?

When profits are outsourced from the country instead of maintained within, a country tends to struggle to maintain an equitable economy. Which means the wealth is heavily concentrated at the top, with little to no wealth distribution to those that do not have a stake in a business enterprise or work for the government. In a sense, the Nigerian state is still operating as a colonized nation because the government exists to maintain the law and order necessary for the international businesses to keep thriving. Those businesses which are contributing to the instability of the nation's economy.

This brings us to the present day End SARs movement and citizen’s demands. While the majority of the demands are rightfully rooted in disbanding SARs and calling on law enforcement to respect rule of law, it is key to note that their demands have expanded. Youth across the country have been demanding more respect for human rights, a deepening of democracy, a revival of educational and health systems, and strong efforts toward job creation.

The government has been a key player in what Adichie refers to as “state-sponsored thuggery,” which occurred the day of the October protest. While the End SARs protests were peaceful and demonstrative of the power of Nigeria’s youth, the government stepped in and paid citizens to act as protestors in order to create a violent portrayal of the otherwise organized protests. Those most socio-economically vulnerable admitted to being paid by the government to join the protests and become violent. This attests to the depth of the problem within Nigeria. Greed, corruption, and a complete disregard for human rights. All of which are a result of and continuation of colonial legacies; not separate from its historical development. In order to move forward, the impacts of racism and colonialism driven by the international economy must be addressed.
